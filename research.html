4 · Find transformed occurrences of your selected passage

Search for transformed occurrences of the entire passage you selected by dragging. Its beginning and end stay fixed. Candidate windows may contain somewhat more or fewer notes and can differ in pitch level, range, or timing. The manual mode preserves your selected reference boundaries while optimizing each candidate endpoint to find a convincing correspondence. Each candidate is reduced to a smoothed time–pitch curve, its average pitch is removed, and its pitch range may be rescaled. We then allow moderate local temporal deformation using a constrained dynamic-time-warping comparison. This is a musical resemblance search, not automatic identification of Bach’s subject.

What does “low resolution” mean here?

Every melodic candidate is represented by 24 samples spread across its notated duration. A short smoothing filter suppresses note-by-note fluctuations. Removing mean pitch makes the comparison invariant to transposition; optionally dividing by pitch-range scale allows approximate interval expansion or compression. Manual matching preserves the selected reference extent and searches variable candidate lengths; adaptive search varies both fragment lengths (and searches every candidate onset); the ending points are chosen by minimizing a regularized contour distance. The selected fragments are shown with their estimated pitch offset, pitch-range ratio and time-scale ratio. Candidates are penalized for very short matches or nearly flat shapes, because these can generate deceptively good scores. Their starting and ending times are normalized. A constrained dynamic-time-warping path tolerates local differences in pacing. Candidates are ranked by normalized contour distance, and strongly overlapping results from the same voice are suppressed. A low score means that the shapes are similar—not that their harmony, rhythm, or formal function are the same.

Limitations: Optimization of fragment boundaries can overfit: the best numerical match does not prove thematic identity. This comparison models one voice at a time, uses pitches and score timing rather than acoustic features, and is not invariant to melodic inversion or reversal. Rests and articulation remain simplified, and no candidate should be called a fugue subject until its rhythmic and contrapuntal context has been checked against the score.

5 · Discover, don't assume, the theme

Experiment A: autonomy

Listen to each vocal part alone. Follow its changes of direction, rhythm and register. Which passages retain melodic character without the other parts?

Experiment B: interaction

Begin with one voice and enable the others successively. Listen for overlapping entrances, imitation and moments when independent lines create a collective gesture.

Experiment C: recognition

Find a gesture that sounds familiar in a second voice. Compare pitch and rhythm. Is it a literal repetition, a transposition, a transformation—or merely a coincidental resemblance?

What is—and isn't—encoded here?

The note events and colors come from the supplied MuseScore document. This prototype does not yet provide validated subject, countersubject, episode, stretto or exposition annotations. A later thematic view will require score-based candidate matching and musical verification. Playback currently uses sustained, organ-like equal-tempered instrumental tones and an adjustable constant tempo, and does not reproduce instrumental accompaniment, ornament interpretation, choir diction, rubato, or dynamics.
