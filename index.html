What are we trying to discover?

In Bach's Kyrie I from the Mass in B minor, five independent vocal lines participate in a large-scale contrapuntal design. We want to find passages that resemble each other, even when sung by different voices or at different pitch levels.

Important: a similar-looking melodic contour is not automatically an occurrence of the fugue subject (soggetto) or countersubject (controsoggetto). First we discover candidates; then we interpret them musically.

4 · What do these similarities mean musically?

Ask yourself: does the resemblance persist when you listen? Are the characteristic intervals and rhythmic accents also similar? Do related passages occur in different voices? Are any of them heard together?

Don't label the results “subject” or “countersubject” yet. Proper identification also requires rhythmic, tonal, and contrapuntal evidence and verification against the score. The algorithm can suggest relationships; it does not determine their compositional function.
How does automatic discovery work? (Optional mathematical explanation)

The current offline proposals were derived from short windows of score notes. Each melodic curve is time-resampled and smoothed. Pitch is centered to remove vertical translation; amplitude normalization reduces sensitivity to pitch-range expansion and compression. A weak onset-pattern comparison contributes rhythmic evidence. High-scoring families are selected partly for recurrence across voices.

For a melody represented by pitch p(t), a simple normalization is z(t) = (p(t) − mean(p)) / max(ε, scale(p)), followed by time resampling. This sacrifices absolute register and some interval information in exchange for transformation tolerance. That is a deliberate modeling choice, not a claim that such information is musically irrelevant.

Scope limitation: these precomputed discovery families use low-resolution contour and a limited rhythmic contribution. They do not yet employ a validated local harmonic/key analysis. The research interface additionally offers an experimental scale-relative matcher.

Why can the computer be wrong?

Many ordinary melodies share an arch or rising–falling outline. Smoothing, transposition tolerance, and flexible time scaling can create false matches. Conversely, a real thematic transformation may not look alike in these coordinates. Listen, compare intervals and rhythm, and consult the notated score before accepting a relationship.
